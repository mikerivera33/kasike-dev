      My story

      The Long Ride Home

      I lost a full scholarship in the middle of a baseball game. Everything I have built since started on the bus ride back.

      By Mike Rivera · @kasike · November 2026 · 6 min read

      We were in Florida for the spring trip, in the middle of a game, when the word came down: finish it, pack up, get on the bus. It was March 2020 and COVID was shutting the country down. Washington was telling everyone to stay home, state after state was making it an order, and campuses were closing one after another. Ours wanted us back to clear out our rooms before classes moved online.

      The drive north took eighteen hours. I had graduated in the top 2 percent of my high school class and accepted a full scholarship to Immaculata University. I was a freshman there, playing college baseball and working toward a double major in criminology and psychology. By the time I carried my things out of the dorm, all of that was already ending. I just hadn’t read the fine print yet.

      The fine print

      My scholarship had a condition. To keep it, I had to be on campus and playing baseball. With the campus closed under the stay-at-home orders and the season cancelled, I could do neither. The scholarship went away, and without it I had to leave school.

      It crushed me. Baseball was what I had organized my life around, and it was gone in an afternoon for reasons that had nothing to do with how I played. I didn’t have time to grieve it properly. I needed a paycheck more than I needed a plan.

      It wasn’t the only thing that landed on me that spring. I wrote about the rest in Long Sleeves.

      Two hundred feet up

      I went back to work alongside my father, in structural steel. I started as a journeyman ironworker and rigging connector. The connector is the one standing on the steel when the crane swings the next piece in, who catches it and makes the first bolts. We built mid-rise and high-rise towers across all five boroughs of New York City, working under mobile cranes, crawler cranes and tower cranes. A year in, I was promoted to foreman.

      Walking a beam two hundred feet above the street teaches things a classroom doesn’t. You check your own work, because the next person is going to stand on it. And you count your crew at the end of the day, because the only good shift is the one everybody walks away from.

      Feet on the ground

      After my time on the beams, I wanted my feet back on the ground. So I taught myself to weld in my backyard and kept at it until I was certified.

      My first welding job was at Wastequip, building industrial dumpsters on an assembly line. It isn’t glamorous work and I’m glad I did it. An assembly line gives you repetitions, and repetitions are how a hand learns. After a year I moved to Delaware Valley Container & Repair as a mobile fabrication specialist and repair welder, driving the tri-state area to fix structural and ornamental steel on site. A different site and a different problem every day.

      A year of that, and I wanted something bigger.

      Second shift

      I found it at Rhoads Industries, at the Philadelphia Navy Yard. Rhoads builds for the U.S. Navy’s nuclear submarine program, and my work there was on the Virginia class and Columbia class submarines. I’m not going to describe that work in any detail. It isn’t mine to share.

      What I can describe is the growth. When I walked in, the whole program had thirty-eight welders on day shift. The company had just landed a contract renewal and a major investment to expand, and the plan was to turn a family-run fabrication shop into one of the major military fabricators on the East Coast. Part of that plan was a second shift. I was on the original night crew: nine welders, one team lead and one manager.

      Three years later, when I left, there were about two hundred welders, fitters, inspectors and other personnel on days, and just under eighty on nights.

      38 → ~280From 38 day-shift welders to about 280 people across two shifts
      18% → 3%Rework on my crew
      10,000+Hours worked in three years
      200+Navy-issued qualifications earned

      I started at the bottom, as an entry-level nuclear welder. I had one rule for myself: nobody in the building was going to outwork me. In just under a year I was a team lead, running a crew of up to fifteen people.

      Eighteen to three

      The number I’m proudest of from those years is a small one. In this kind of work, every weld is inspected, and anything that doesn’t pass has to be fixed and inspected again. That’s called rework. When I took over my crew, about 18 percent of our production work was coming back. We got it down to 3 percent, and we did it with zero lost-time injuries.

      There was no trick to it. I trained my people on the job, held the same standard every shift, and stayed on the tools myself.

      Seven months after I made team lead, I was promoted again, to operations supervisor. I was twenty-three. As far as anyone there could tell me, I was the youngest person holding that job in the program at the time.

      I ran production schedules, planned and assigned the work, worked on budgets, coordinated with upper management and government officials, and trained the welders who were struggling. I also kept doing my share of the hands-on specialty work and shipfitting.

      In three years I put in a little over ten thousand hours. I also earned more than two hundred Navy-issued qualifications: stainless steel welding, high-capacity and complex rigging, scaffold building, and weld inspection by visual, liquid penetrant, magnetic particle and radiographic methods, among many others. Each one was a kind of job Rhoads could take on in-house instead of sending out to another shop. I collected them because I love learning how to do things. That has been true at every job I’ve had.

      Starting over, again

      Then came the government shutdown, and I was let go.

      This time I knew what to do when a door closed. My father was starting LJM Homes, a small family-run commercial real estate investment firm, and I went back to school so I could help him build it. I completed a professional certificate program in real estate and private equity at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. Today I run all of the underwriting and deal vetting for the firm, and I have underwritten more than $150 million in multifamily acquisitions.

      Underwriting turned out to be closer to welding than I expected. Both come down to finding the flaw before it gets buried under something expensive.

      Building a firm with my father made me want to build one of my own. So my fiancée, Alexa Fisher, and I started Aurevon Ventures and Aurevon Labs. We work across business consulting, operations management consulting, digital products, a digital community, Web3 products, and AI agent implementation and strategy.

      Kasike

      Online I go by kasike. It’s a Taíno word, the one the Spanish wrote down as cacique, and it means chief. My family is Puerto Rican. When I started building a presence of my own, I dug down into those roots and took the name.

      I lost a scholarship in the middle of a game. Since then I have connected steel over New York, welded for the Navy’s submarines, run a shift, underwritten deals, and started a company with the woman I’m going to marry. None of it was the plan I had at eighteen. All of it came from the same two habits: learn the next skill, and don’t let anyone outwork you.

      I’m still on that ride. If you want to see where it goes next, come along.

      Long Sleeves

      For most of my life I covered my skin so nobody would see it. This is the part of my story I left out of the first one.

      By Mike Rivera · @kasike · November 2026 · 3 min read

      Growing up, I wore long sleeves most of the time. It wasn’t a style. I had severe eczema from my neck down, and my skin was red and inflamed nearly all the time. To anyone who didn’t know better, it looked like hives or chickenpox. Long sleeves kept people from seeing it, and they kept me from the embarrassment of being seen.

      Thirty specialists

      I saw more than thirty different specialists. For years, nothing got it under control.

      Then, at seventeen, I was approved for the under-18 human trials of a biologic drug for eczema that was new at the time, called Dupixent. I was told I was the first person in the United States approved for them.

      For me it was a miracle drug. It worked wonders, and it seemed to fix every medical issue I had.

      It also cost about $6,000 a month out of pocket, and that was with insurance.

      The same week

      I have already written about the spring of 2020 in The Long Ride Home. COVID sent us home from Florida, the campus closed under the stay-at-home orders, and my scholarship went with it. I left two things out.

      Right after we were sent home, my grandmother passed away. She was living with us, and she and I were very close.

      That same week, I was diagnosed with epilepsy.

      I have been battling it, coping with it and living with it ever since, and I depend on medication to do that.

      What coverage is worth

      While I was at Rhoads Industries, my health coverage came through my employer. When Rhoads and I parted ways, the coverage went with the job. Since then, with no job and no insurance, I haven’t been able to afford the out-of-pocket costs on my own.

      I’m not telling this for sympathy. I’m telling it because it is part of the honest answer to why I work the way I do.

      Another reason

      The scholarship, the long sleeves, the specialists, my grandmother, the diagnosis, the bill. Each one is another reason I have worked as hard as I have over the years, and another reason I’m as determined as I am.

      For most of my life I covered up what I was dealing with. This is me rolling the sleeves up.

      The 3% Standard

      Every weld I made for a Navy submarine was inspected by someone whose job was to find the flaw. In most businesses, nobody inspects the AI. That gap is where the money goes.

      By Mike Rivera · @kasike · November 2026 · 8 min read

      Every weld I made for a Navy submarine was examined by someone whose job was to find something wrong with it.

      I can’t walk you through the work itself, but the idea of weld inspection is public and simple. First a trained inspector looks at the weld. Then it can be tested with a dye that seeps into cracks too fine to see, with magnetic particles that gather where the metal is broken, or with X-rays that show what is inside. If the weld fails, it gets fixed and inspected again. That second pass is called rework, and everyone in the shop knows how much of it belongs to them.

      When I became a team lead at Rhoads Industries, working on the Virginia class and Columbia class submarine programs, about 18 percent of my crew’s production work was coming back as rework. We brought it down to 3 percent.

      I build AI systems for businesses now. I want to tell you what that number has to do with the agent you are about to put in charge of your inbox.

      Nobody inspects the agent

      Here is the first thing I noticed when I moved from the shipyard to software. Where I came from, work wasn’t finished until someone other than the person who did it had signed for it. In an office, an AI agent drafts the email, updates the record, posts the content or prices the job, and the output goes straight out the door. Nobody looks at it. People call that automation. In a fabrication shop we would call it shipping uninspected work.

      The results are showing up in the numbers. In June 2025, Gartner predicted that more than 40 percent of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by the end of 2027, and named escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls as the reasons.1 S&P Global Market Intelligence surveyed more than a thousand companies and found that 42 percent had abandoned most of their AI initiatives in 2025, up from 17 percent the year before.2 A RAND study from 2024, built on interviews with 65 experienced data scientists and engineers, reported that by some estimates more than 80 percent of AI projects fail, twice the rate of IT projects without AI.3

      You will also hear that 95 percent of corporate AI pilots fail. I’d leave that one alone. It comes from a 2025 MIT report whose methods have been seriously questioned, and the numbers above make the point without it.4

      What none of these studies can tell you is how often the agents are simply wrong. I went looking for a reliable published figure on how much AI output in business has to be redone, and I didn’t find one. In the shop I came from, not knowing your rework rate would be unthinkable. Most companies running AI agents can’t tell you theirs, because nobody is counting.

      Rework is where the money goes

      Quality engineers have a name for what sloppy work costs: the cost of poor quality. Researchers cited by the Institute of Industrial and Systems Engineers estimate that it averages around 15 percent of sales in manufacturing and around 30 percent in service organizations.5 Rework is a big piece of that. You pay for the same job twice, once to do it and once to fix it.

      In a fabrication shop, rework is loud. You can hear the grinder taking a bad weld back out. In an office it makes no sound at all. It looks like an employee quietly rewriting the proposal the AI drafted. It looks like a customer who got a wrong answer and didn’t bother to complain, or a spreadsheet that is slightly off for three months before anyone notices.

      The pitch for AI is hours saved. Hours saved on the first pass mean nothing if the work comes back.

      The number that matters is how often the work is right the first time. Quality people call it first pass yield: the percentage of units that go through a process and meet the standard without being scrapped, rerun, retested or repaired.6 Ask the person selling you an AI agent what its first pass yield is on your work. If they don’t have an answer, you will be the one measuring it, with your own customers.

      Run the numbers

      Here is an illustration, not a client. Say a small company lets an agent draft 200 customer replies a week. At an 18 percent rework rate, 36 of those replies are wrong in a way that matters.

      If someone reviews every reply, those 36 get caught and rewritten, and much of the time you thought you saved goes to cleanup. If nobody reviews them, 36 wrong answers go out every week with your name at the bottom.

      At 3 percent, it’s 6. Six is a number one person can catch, fix and learn from before lunch. The difference between an agent that helps and an agent that quietly costs you customers is those other thirty replies a week.

      How a crew gets from 18 to 3

      I didn’t get my crew’s rework down by buying anything. What changed was how we worked, and it came down to four things.

      The standard came first. In inspected work, what counts as a pass is settled before anyone strikes an arc. Nobody argues afterward about whether a weld is good enough. It meets the standard or it comes out.

      Training came before blame. When a welder on my crew was struggling, I trained them on the job. A failed weld told me what to teach next. I have come to believe that most rework is a training problem before it is a talent problem.

      I stayed on the tools. As a team lead, and later as a supervisor, I kept doing my share of the hands-on specialty work. The standard I enforced was one my crew watched me meet.

      Safety and quality were the same habit. We did all of this with zero lost-time injuries. I don’t think that is a coincidence. A crew that is careful about one thing tends to be careful about everything.

      The 3% standard for AI

      None of that is specific to steel. If you are putting an AI agent to work in your business, this is the order I’d do it in.

      1. Write the pass/fail test before you build. For each task the agent will do, write down what a correct result looks like, in terms a person can check in under a minute. If you can’t write it down, the agent can’t hit it.
      2. Inspect everything at first. For the first few weeks, a person checks every output before it leaves the building. It is slow, and it’s supposed to be. You are qualifying the process, the same way a welder has to qualify before touching the real work.
      3. Count the rework. Keep one number. Of everything the agent produced this week, what share went out without a human having to fix it? Put that number where the whole team can see it.
      4. Fix the cause, not the output. When something fails, don’t just correct that one email. Find out why it failed. Usually it is a missing instruction, bad source data, or a task nobody ever defined clearly. Fix that, or you’ll be correcting the same mistake every week.
      5. Earn the right to stop looking. Once the number holds, move from checking everything to checking a sample. If it slips, go back to checking everything.
      6. Keep a signature on what matters. Anything that commits money, makes a promise to a customer or touches someone’s safety gets a human sign-off, however good the numbers look.

      People push back on the second step. If a person has to check everything, what did the agent save? At the start, not much. But nobody inspected my welds because they thought I was a bad welder. They inspected them because the cost of being wrong was too high to take anyone’s word for it, mine included.

      The bar I’d hold a workflow to is the one my crew reached: no more than 3 percent coming back. Three percent isn’t a law of nature. It was one crew’s number on one program. But it gives you something most AI projects never have, which is a line you can be above or below.

      Where the comparison stops

      I don’t want to oversell this. A weld and an email are different kinds of work, and the comparison has limits.

      A weld is judged against a written standard, and two inspectors will mostly agree on whether it passes. Much of what we ask AI to do is judgment: tone, priority, what to leave out. The test is harder to write, and some tasks never get a clean one. Those are the tasks to keep a person on.

      A qualified welder’s mistakes are mostly the familiar kind. An agent can do a task correctly ninety-nine times and fail the hundredth in a way nobody has seen before, because something changed upstream. That is why I would never stop sampling completely.

      And inspection costs time, which not all work deserves. Nobody needs a sign-off on a meeting summary that only they will read. Put your inspection where a mistake is expensive, and be honest with yourself that the rest is running unchecked.

      What the trades already know

      The Navy has said the submarine industrial base needs to hire more than 140,000 workers within the next decade.7 I think about that number a lot, because it says something the technology world tends to forget. The most advanced machines this country builds still depend on people who can do careful work and prove that it’s right.

      AI doesn’t change that. It raises the stakes. An agent can turn out more work in an hour than a crew can in a week, which means it can turn out more bad work too. The companies that come out ahead will treat an agent the way a good shop treats any new hand: trained, inspected, measured, and trusted exactly as far as its record supports.

      I learned that on night shift at the Navy Yard. I use it every day at a keyboard.
